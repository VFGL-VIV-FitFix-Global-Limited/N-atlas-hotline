# Yam Farming in Nigeria

## Overview

Nigeria is the world's largest producer of yams, accounting for over 70% of global production. Yam is a staple food and a culturally important crop, especially in southern and central Nigeria. Yam farming can be very profitable but requires significant labour and investment.

## Types of Yam

- **White yam (Dioscorea rotundata):** Most common and popular in Nigeria, used for pounded yam, boiled yam, and fried yam
- **Water yam (Dioscorea alata):** Grows well in wetter areas, good yield, used for porridge and frying
- **Yellow yam (Dioscorea cayenensis):** Similar to white yam, yellow flesh
- **Aerial yam (Dioscorea bulbifera):** Produces tubers on the vine, less common
- **Three-leaf yam (Dioscorea dumetorum):** Bitter if not processed properly, but high-yielding

## Improved Varieties

IITA and NRCRI (National Root Crops Research Institute, Umudike) have developed improved varieties with:
- Higher yield (20-30 tonnes per hectare vs 10-12 tonnes for local)
- Earlier maturity
- Disease resistance
- Better tuber quality

Check with your state ADP or NRCRI for recommended varieties in your area.

## Planting

### When to Plant
- Plant between November and March (dry season planting)
- Some areas plant up to April-May
- Early planting gives better yields

### Land Preparation
1. Clear the land
2. Make yam mounds or ridges
3. Mounds: 60-90 cm high, spaced 1m x 1m (about 10,000 mounds per hectare)
4. Ridges: 60-90 cm high, 1m apart, plant 30-50 cm apart on the ridge
5. Mounds are more traditional; ridges are faster to prepare and easier to mechanise

### Planting Material (Seed Yam)
- Use whole small tubers (seed yams, 150-300g) or cut setts (200-500g pieces from larger tubers)
- Treat cut setts with wood ash or fungicide to prevent rot
- Allow cut setts to heal (dry in shade for 2-3 days) before planting
- Plant one seed yam or sett per mound or ridge position
- Plant 10-15 cm deep

### Staking
- Yam vines need support to climb — staking increases yield by 30-50%
- Use wooden stakes (1.5-2m long), one per mound
- Alternatively, use trellis systems (stakes with connecting ropes or wires)
- Guide the vine onto the stake as it grows

## Crop Management

### Weeding
- Weed 2-3 times during the growing season
- First weeding: 4-6 weeks after sprouting
- Second weeding: 10-12 weeks
- Keep the area around mounds clear

### Fertiliser
- Apply NPK 15:15:15 at 300-400 kg per hectare
- Apply 6-8 weeks after sprouting, in a ring around the mound (not directly on the plant)
- Organic manure is also effective — apply before planting or at mound making
- Poultry manure: 5-10 tonnes per hectare

### Mulching
- Cover mounds with dry grass, leaves, or crop residue after planting
- Mulching protects the seed yam from heat, conserves moisture, and suppresses weeds
- Remove mulch once the yam sprouts and grows

## Pests and Diseases

### Major Pests
- **Yam beetles:** Damage tuber skin, creating entry points for disease. Use recommended insecticides.
- **Nematodes:** Cause tuber cracks and reduce quality. Rotate crops and use clean planting material.
- **Rodents:** Dig up and eat tubers. Clear vegetation around the farm.
- **Scale insects and mealybugs:** Attack stored yam tubers. Treat seed yams before planting.

### Major Diseases
- **Yam anthracnose:** Dark spots on leaves, causes leaf death. Use resistant varieties; apply fungicides.
- **Yam mosaic virus:** Leaf distortion and mosaic patterns. Use virus-free planting material.
- **Tuber rot (storage):** Fungal and bacterial rot during storage. Handle tubers carefully; store in well-ventilated barns.

## Harvesting

- **First harvest (milking):** Some varieties allow partial harvest at 5-7 months — cut a piece of tuber while the plant is still growing, then cover the remaining tuber with soil
- **Main harvest:** 8-12 months after planting, when leaves turn yellow and dry
- Dig carefully to avoid damaging tubers
- Handle tubers gently — damaged tubers rot quickly

## Storage

- Store in a well-ventilated yam barn (traditional structure with shelves or hanging yams)
- Inspect stored yams regularly for rot and remove affected tubers
- Treat with recommended storage chemicals if available
- Good ventilation and cool temperatures reduce losses
- Storage losses can be 20-30% or more — sell or process early if possible

## Economics

- **Cost of production:** ₦400,000-₦800,000 per hectare (yam is the most expensive crop to produce due to seed cost and labour)
- **Seed yam cost:** This is the biggest expense — seed yams can cost ₦200,000-₦400,000 per hectare
- **Expected yield:** 15-30 tonnes per hectare with improved varieties
- **Selling price:** ₦100,000-₦250,000 per tonne (varies greatly by season — highest prices in July-September when stocks are low)
- **Revenue potential:** ₦1,500,000-₦5,000,000+ per hectare

## Tips

- Yam farming is capital-intensive but very profitable
- The cost of seed yam is the biggest challenge — consider producing your own seed yams (minisett technique: cut seed yams into 25-30g pieces, nursery-raise them, then transplant)
- Early planting gives better yields
- Staking is worth the cost — it pays for itself in increased yield
- Do not plant diseased or damaged seed yams
- Consider yam flour processing for value addition
- Prices are highest in the "hunger season" (July-September) — time your sales if you can store
- Join a yam farmers' cooperative for better access to planting material and markets
