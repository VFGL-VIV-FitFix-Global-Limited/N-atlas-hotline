# Diabetes in Nigeria

## What is Diabetes?

Diabetes is a condition where the body cannot properly use or produce insulin, leading to high blood sugar levels. If not managed, it can damage the heart, kidneys, eyes, nerves, and blood vessels. Diabetes is a growing health problem in Nigeria, affecting millions of people.

## Types of Diabetes

### Type 1 Diabetes
- The body does not produce insulin at all
- Usually starts in childhood or young adulthood
- Requires daily insulin injections
- Less common in Nigeria

### Type 2 Diabetes
- The body does not use insulin properly or does not produce enough
- Most common type in Nigeria (over 90% of cases)
- Often linked to lifestyle: obesity, lack of exercise, unhealthy diet
- Can be managed with diet, exercise, and medication
- Some people eventually need insulin

### Gestational Diabetes
- Occurs during pregnancy
- Usually goes away after delivery, but increases risk of developing Type 2 diabetes later
- Requires monitoring and management during pregnancy

## Symptoms

- Frequent urination (especially at night)
- Excessive thirst
- Unexplained weight loss
- Constant hunger
- Blurred vision
- Slow-healing wounds and sores
- Frequent infections
- Tingling or numbness in hands and feet
- Fatigue and weakness

Many people with Type 2 diabetes have no symptoms for years — regular screening is important.

## Risk Factors

- Family history of diabetes
- Overweight or obese
- Age over 40 (but can occur at any age)
- Sedentary lifestyle (lack of exercise)
- High blood pressure
- History of gestational diabetes
- Unhealthy diet high in sugar and processed foods

## Diagnosis

- **Fasting Blood Sugar (FBS):** Blood test taken after not eating for 8-12 hours
  - Normal: below 5.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL)
  - Pre-diabetes: 5.6-6.9 mmol/L (100-125 mg/dL)
  - Diabetes: 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL) or above
- **Random Blood Sugar:** Can be tested at any time
  - Diabetes: 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) or above with symptoms
- **HbA1c:** Measures average blood sugar over 2-3 months
  - Normal: below 5.7%
  - Pre-diabetes: 5.7-6.4%
  - Diabetes: 6.5% or above

Get tested at any hospital, health centre, or diagnostic laboratory.

## Management

### Diet
- Eat balanced meals at regular times
- Reduce sugar and sugary drinks (soft drinks, malt, fruit juice)
- Reduce starchy foods — choose smaller portions of rice, garri, fufu, pounded yam
- Eat more vegetables (ewedu, ugu, spinach, okra, garden egg)
- Choose whole grains: ofada rice, whole wheat, brown rice, oats
- Eat lean protein: fish, chicken, beans, eggs
- Avoid fried foods — boil, grill, or steam instead
- Eat fruits in moderation (avoid very sweet fruits in excess)
- Reduce salt intake

### Exercise
- Aim for at least 30 minutes of moderate exercise most days
- Walking, cycling, swimming, dancing, and farming all count
- Exercise helps your body use insulin better and lowers blood sugar
- Start slowly if you are not used to exercising

### Medication
- Take all prescribed medication regularly and as directed
- Common medications: Metformin, Glimepiride, Glibenclamide
- Some people need insulin injections
- Do not stop medication because you feel better — diabetes is a lifelong condition
- Do not use herbal mixtures as a replacement for prescribed medication

### Monitoring
- Check your blood sugar regularly (your doctor will tell you how often)
- Home glucometers are available at pharmacies (₦5,000-₦15,000)
- Keep a record of your readings to share with your doctor
- Regular check-ups: every 3 months or as advised

## Complications

If diabetes is not well managed, it can cause:
- Heart disease and stroke
- Kidney failure (requiring dialysis)
- Eye damage and blindness (diabetic retinopathy)
- Nerve damage (numbness, pain, tingling in feet and hands)
- Foot ulcers and infections (can lead to amputation)
- Erectile dysfunction in men
- Increased risk of infections

## Foot Care for Diabetics

- Wash and inspect your feet daily
- Dry between toes carefully
- Wear comfortable, well-fitting shoes — never walk barefoot
- Cut toenails straight across, not too short
- Do not use razor blades to remove calluses
- See a doctor immediately for any wound, sore, or colour change on your feet
- Diabetic foot wounds heal slowly and can become very serious

## When to Go to the Hospital

- Blood sugar very high (above 16.7 mmol/L or 300 mg/dL)
- Blood sugar very low (below 3.9 mmol/L or 70 mg/dL): symptoms include shaking, sweating, confusion, dizziness
- Wound on foot or leg that is not healing
- Chest pain or difficulty breathing
- Vision changes
- Severe vomiting or confusion

## Tips

- Diabetes is not a death sentence — millions of people live well with it
- Take your medication faithfully and keep your appointments
- Do not believe claims that herbal mixtures can cure diabetes — there is no cure, but it can be managed
- Join a diabetes support group in your community or online
- Carry a form of identification that says you have diabetes
