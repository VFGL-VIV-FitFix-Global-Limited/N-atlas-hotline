# Pregnancy and Antenatal Care in Nigeria

## Why Antenatal Care Matters

Antenatal care (ANC) is the health care you receive during pregnancy. Regular ANC visits help detect and prevent problems early, keeping both mother and baby safe. Nigeria has one of the highest maternal mortality rates in the world — many of these deaths are preventable with proper care.

## When to Start Antenatal Care

- Start as soon as you know you are pregnant — ideally in the first 3 months (first trimester)
- The World Health Organisation recommends at least 8 ANC visits during pregnancy
- Visit schedule: monthly in the first 6 months, fortnightly in months 7-8, and weekly in the last month

## Where to Go

- Primary Health Centres (PHCs) — available in every Local Government Area, often free or very low cost
- General hospitals
- Teaching hospitals
- Private hospitals and clinics
- Registered midwifery centres

Government primary health centres offer free or subsidised ANC in many states.

## What Happens at ANC Visits

### First Visit
- Medical history and examination
- Blood pressure check
- Blood tests: blood group, genotype (AA, AS, SS), HIV, hepatitis B, haemoglobin level
- Urine test
- Weight and height measurement
- Estimated due date calculation
- Tetanus toxoid (TT) vaccination
- Iron and folic acid supplements
- Health education

### Follow-Up Visits
- Blood pressure check
- Weight check
- Urine test
- Baby's heartbeat check (from about 5 months)
- Baby's position check (later months)
- IPTp for malaria prevention (Sulfadoxine-Pyrimethamine)
- Iron and folic acid supplements
- Any concerns or symptoms discussed

## Important Tests During Pregnancy

- **Genotype test** — Know your genotype and your partner's. If both are AS, there is a 25% chance your baby will be SS (sickle cell). Discuss with your doctor.
- **HIV test** — If positive, treatment (PMTCT) can prevent the baby from getting HIV
- **Blood group and Rhesus factor** — Rhesus-negative mothers may need special injections
- **Haemoglobin level** — To check for anaemia, which is very common in Nigerian pregnancies

## Nutrition During Pregnancy

### What to Eat
- Eat balanced meals: protein (beans, fish, eggs, meat), carbohydrates (rice, yam, garri), vegetables and fruits
- Eat dark green leafy vegetables (spinach, ugu, ewedu) for iron and folic acid
- Drink plenty of clean water
- Eat fruits daily (oranges, bananas, pawpaw, watermelon)
- Include milk, yoghurt, or other dairy for calcium

### What to Avoid
- Alcohol — no amount is safe during pregnancy
- Smoking and exposure to smoke
- Raw or undercooked meat and eggs
- Excessive caffeine (limit to 1-2 cups of coffee or tea per day)
- Self-medication — do not take any medicine without asking your doctor or midwife
- Herbal concoctions (agbo) — these can be dangerous and cause miscarriage or harm the baby

### Supplements
- Take iron and folic acid tablets as given at ANC
- Take folic acid from before conception if possible (prevents birth defects)
- Take calcium supplements if recommended

## Danger Signs in Pregnancy

Go to the hospital IMMEDIATELY if you have:
- Heavy bleeding from the vagina
- Severe headache that does not go away
- Blurred vision or seeing spots
- Severe swelling of face, hands, or feet
- High fever
- Severe abdominal pain
- Water breaking before 37 weeks
- Baby not moving for more than 12 hours (after 5 months)
- Convulsions or fits
- Difficulty breathing

These could be signs of eclampsia, placenta problems, or other emergencies.

## Preparing for Delivery

### Birth Preparedness Plan
- Decide where you will deliver (hospital is safest)
- Know how you will get to the hospital (arrange transport in advance)
- Save money for delivery costs
- Identify your blood group and have at least 2 people ready to donate blood if needed
- Pack a delivery bag: baby clothes, sanitary pads, your ANC card, clean wrapper, toiletries
- Know the danger signs and when to go to the hospital

### Where to Deliver
- Deliver in a health facility — not at home
- Skilled birth attendants (doctors, midwives, nurses) can handle emergencies
- If you plan to deliver at a traditional birth attendant (TBA), know that they cannot handle complications — consider a health facility instead

## After Delivery (Postnatal Care)

- Stay at the health facility for observation (at least 24 hours)
- Start breastfeeding within 1 hour of birth
- Exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months (no water, no formula, no pap)
- Attend postnatal check-ups: within 48 hours, at 1-2 weeks, and at 6 weeks
- Family planning counselling is available at postnatal visits
- Watch for postnatal danger signs: heavy bleeding, fever, foul-smelling discharge, breast problems

## Family Planning After Delivery

After delivery, discuss family planning with your health provider. Options include:
- Implants (Jadelle, Implanon)
- Injectables (Depo-Provera)
- IUD (copper or hormonal)
- Pills
- Condoms
- Lactational Amenorrhoea Method (LAM) — for the first 6 months if exclusively breastfeeding

Family planning is available free or at low cost at most government health centres.

## Tips

- Never skip your ANC appointments
- Take your iron and folic acid tablets every day
- Sleep under a treated mosquito net
- Rest well and avoid heavy lifting
- Do not take any medicine unless prescribed by your doctor or midwife
- If you cannot afford care, ask about free maternal health programmes in your state
